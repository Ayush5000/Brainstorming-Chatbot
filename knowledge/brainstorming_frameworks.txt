BRAINSTORMING FUNDAMENTALS

Brainstorming is a creative problem-solving process used to
generate many possible ideas around a specific topic, problem,
goal, user need or opportunity.

A good brainstorming process should not immediately jump to
random project ideas. It should first understand the topic or
problem and then explore different angles.

The basic process is:

1. Define the topic or problem.
2. Understand the users and their needs.
3. Explore the current situation and existing solutions.
4. Identify gaps and opportunities.
5. Generate multiple possible approaches.
6. Combine, modify or challenge ideas.
7. Evaluate promising ideas.
8. Improve the strongest ideas.

IMPORTANT BRAINSTORMING PRINCIPLE

Brainstorming should be anchored around a topic, problem or goal.

For example:

Topic:
College student productivity.

Possible exploration areas:

- What problems do students face?
- Why do these problems happen?
- What solutions already exist?
- What is missing from existing solutions?
- What could be automated?
- What could AI improve?
- What completely different approaches could work?
- What assumptions can be challenged?

The answers to these questions become the raw material
for generating ideas.

PROBLEM EXPLORATION

Useful problem exploration questions include:

- What is the biggest difficulty?
- Who experiences the problem?
- When does the problem happen?
- Why does the problem happen?
- What makes the problem difficult?
- What are users currently doing?
- What is frustrating about current solutions?
- What part of the process is inefficient?
- What part is expensive, slow or complicated?

USER-CENTERED BRAINSTORMING

Ideas should consider the people affected by the problem.

Explore:

- Who is the target user?
- What does the user want?
- What does the user struggle with?
- What does the user currently use?
- What would make the experience easier?
- What would save time?
- What would reduce effort?
- What would create more value?

EXISTING SOLUTIONS

Before generating ideas, understand existing solutions.

Explore:

- What solutions already exist?
- What do they do well?
- What do they do poorly?
- What important need remains unsolved?
- What users still complain about?
- What part of the experience can be improved?

OPPORTUNITY IDENTIFICATION

An opportunity is a useful gap between the current situation
and a better possible situation.

Look for:

- unmet user needs
- inefficient processes
- expensive processes
- repetitive tasks
- confusing experiences
- inaccessible services
- unused data
- opportunities for automation
- opportunities for personalization
- opportunities created by new technology

IDEA GENERATION

Strong ideas should be:

- relevant to the topic
- connected to a real problem
- meaningfully different from each other
- understandable
- feasible enough to explore
- capable of creating useful value

Do not generate random ideas only to increase the number of ideas.

IDEA COMBINATION

Two or more ideas can sometimes be combined.

Ask:

- Can two approaches work together?
- Can an existing solution be combined with a new technology?
- Can two user needs be solved by one system?
- Can one idea become a feature of another?

WHAT-IF THINKING

"What if" questions help break normal assumptions.

Examples:

- What if the user did not have to perform this step?
- What if this process were completely automated?
- What if the solution were designed for beginners?
- What if the solution had to work with almost no budget?
- What if the solution had to work offline?
- What if the solution had to serve thousands of users?
- What if AI handled the repetitive parts?

CONSTRAINT-BASED BRAINSTORMING

Constraints can create better ideas.

Examples:

- limited budget
- limited time
- limited hardware
- limited internet access
- beginner users
- small team
- privacy requirements
- scalability requirements

A constraint should be treated as a design condition rather
than simply an obstacle.

EVALUATING IDEAS

Promising ideas can be evaluated using:

Innovation:
How different or novel is the idea?

Feasibility:
How realistic is it to build?

Impact:
How valuable could it be for users?

Difficulty:
How difficult would implementation be?

A strong idea does not necessarily have the highest innovation.
A balanced idea with good impact and feasibility can be stronger.

BRAINSTORMING OUTPUT

A useful brainstorming result should contain:

- generated ideas
- short explanation of each idea
- strongest ideas
- evaluation
- weaknesses
- improvements
- possible next steps
